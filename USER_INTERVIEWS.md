- Role: Freelance Developer
- Company/Stage: Independent freelancer working with small startup clients

---

## AI Stack

- ChatGPT
- Cursor
- Claude

Estimated monthly spend:
~$70/month

---

## Direct Quotes

> “I completely forgot I was still paying for Claude.”

> “We sometimes share one ChatGPT account across teammates.”

> “Enterprise pricing feels excessive for tiny teams.”

> “I wish I could see yearly AI spending instead of monthly subscriptions separately.”

---

## Most Surprising Insight

Rahul mentioned that his team occasionally shares ChatGPT accounts informally to reduce costs. This highlighted how even technically skilled users often manage AI subscriptions in ad-hoc ways instead of through structured procurement processes.

---

## What This Changed In My Product

This interview reinforced the importance of:
- highlighting duplicate subscriptions,
- surfacing yearly savings instead of only monthly savings,
- and making recommendations feel financially practical for small teams.

It also pushed me toward emphasizing “overspending visibility” rather than only plan optimization.

## Most Surprising Insight

Aman explained that the biggest issue was not necessarily pricing itself, but lack of centralized visibility. Different teammates subscribed independently, making it difficult to understand total AI spend across the company.

---

## What This Changed In My Product

This interview influenced me to think beyond simple plan recommendations.

It highlighted future opportunities for:
- centralized team dashboards,
- subscription ownership tracking,
- and broader AI spend management workflows.

It also validated the idea that AI tooling fragmentation is becoming a real operational problem for startups.

## Most Surprising Insight

Ranjit viewed AI subscriptions similarly to entertainment subscriptions — easy to start, difficult to track over time, and emotionally justified despite unclear ROI.

This was interesting because it suggested that AI spend optimization is partly a behavioral problem, not only a pricing problem.

---

## What This Changed In My Product

This conversation pushed me toward:
- simpler recommendation explanations,
- clearer savings visibility,
- and lightweight audit experiences that do not overwhelm users with financial terminology.

It also reinforced the importance of trust and clarity in the audit output.

---

# Common Patterns Across Interviews

## 1. AI Spend Visibility Is Poor

None of the participants tracked total AI spending accurately across tools and teams. Most estimated costs loosely rather than using formal tracking systems.

---

## 2. Subscription Overlap Is Common

Several users paid for overlapping tools with partially duplicated functionality, especially across ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.

---

## 3. Users Forget Active Subscriptions

A recurring pattern was inactive or underused subscriptions remaining active for months after initial experimentation.

---

## 4. Small Teams Rarely Need Enterprise Plans

Participants consistently felt that higher-tier plans often exceeded their actual usage requirements.

---

## 5. Trust Is Essential

All participants showed some skepticism toward automated financial recommendations. Users wanted recommendations to feel transparent, practical, and realistic rather than overly aggressive.

---

# Product Direction Changes After Interviews

The interviews shifted the product direction toward:

- clearer savings explanations,
- more transparent recommendation logic,
- stronger emphasis on yearly spend visibility,
- duplicate subscription detection,
- and future potential for centralized AI spend dashboards.

The conversations also reinforced that the product should remain lightweight and simple rather than becoming a complicated finance tool.
